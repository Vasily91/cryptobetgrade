Sportsbooks that confiscate winnings for value betting: documented cases

Limiting a sharp bettor is ordinary risk management. Voiding bets or confiscating winnings the sportsbook already accepted is something else — and players deserve to know the difference before they decide where to bet.

Sportsbooks limiting winning players is nothing new. What is more controversial is what happens when a sportsbook goes beyond limiting an account and voids bets or confiscates winnings that have already been earned.

We reviewed a number of publicly documented disputes where players were accused of value betting, arbitrage, delayed-odds exploitation or similar betting behaviour. The cases show very different approaches. Some sportsbooks simply restrict the player. Others return the original deposit but remove winnings. In some cases, the entire disputed balance is withheld.

Value betting is not arbitrage

The distinction matters.

Arbitrage betting involves covering different outcomes at odds that create a guaranteed profit regardless of the result. Value betting simply means betting when a player believes the offered odds are higher than the true probability of the outcome would justify — there is no guaranteed profit, and the bet can still lose.

Nevertheless, the two terms are sometimes grouped together in sportsbook investigations, often under broader labels such as "advantage play" or "sportsbook abuse." The real controversy begins when these classifications are used not just to restrict future betting, but to cancel winnings from bets the sportsbook had already accepted.

A recurring pattern: the sportsbook provider

One feature appears repeatedly in these disputes: the decision often involves a third-party sportsbook provider or risk-management system rather than the operator itself.

In a public Betpanda dispute, for example, the operator said its sportsbook provider had identified "value betting and/or arbitrage betting." Betpanda also stated that it had not received the specific bets that triggered the provider's conclusion. Similar provider involvement appears in disputes involving other operators in the table above.

If winnings are confiscated because of a provider's risk assessment, should the player be shown which bets actually triggered that assessment?

A generic statement that an account was flagged for "arbitrage" or "abuse" gives the player very little ability to understand or challenge the decision.

Not every flag results in confiscation

The recent Degen case provides a useful counterexample.

What happened

Reviewed for "abuse/arbitrage" — balance paid in full

A sportsbook account was reviewed for more than 24 hours after support referred to an "abuse/arbitrage" investigation. Degen eventually stated that its Sportsbook Rules had been violated and warned that continuing the behaviour could affect future withdrawals. However, the full balance was paid.

The player has requested clarification regarding the specific behaviour that triggered the warning, but there is no outstanding financial dispute. See Degen's profile.

This demonstrates an important point: identifying betting activity as undesirable does not necessarily require confiscating previously earned winnings.

Limiting and confiscating are fundamentally different

Sportsbooks have every commercial incentive to manage sharp action. They can reduce limits, restrict markets or stop accepting future bets from a player. But confiscation is different: the sportsbook has already offered the odds and accepted the player's wager. If the bet subsequently wins, removing the winnings requires a stronger justification than simply deciding the player is no longer profitable to accept.

Cases involving multi-accounting, manipulated events, bonus abuse or technical exploitation are also materially different and should not automatically be grouped together with ordinary value betting. That distinction is why we record an operator's stated reason and the eventual complaint outcome separately, rather than folding every account restriction into one bucket.

The transparency question

We don't treat a player's allegation as proof that a sportsbook acted improperly. Operator responses, mediator decisions and resolved cases matter, and several of the cases above were ultimately resolved in the operator's favour or with a partial payout.

But when winnings are confiscated for betting behaviour, we believe players should at minimum be able to determine:

  • What rule was violated?
  • What behaviour triggered it?
  • Does that rule clearly allow the sportsbook to confiscate winnings — not just restrict future play?

A sportsbook deciding it no longer wants a sharp bettor's action is ordinary risk management. A sportsbook accepting the bets and later removing the winnings is something players deserve to know about before they decide where to bet.
